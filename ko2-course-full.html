Lesson 1.1

Controls Reference

Everything you need to know about the physical layout — fast. Refer back here when something's unclear later.

The Controls
■
12 pads — play sounds, enter steps, type numbers. Pressure sensitive: hit harder for a louder hit. The labels printed above them change meaning depending on which mode button you're holding.
■
Groups A–D — four independent lanes. Think A = drums, B = bass, C = chords, D = extras. Each holds 99 patterns and 12 sounds. All four play simultaneously.
■
– and + — navigate steps, patterns, scenes, and most menus. Hold a group button + press – / + to switch patterns within that group.
■
Knob X / Knob Y — context-sensitive. What they adjust depends on the active mode. Hold SHIFT while turning for finer control.
■
Fader — group volume by default. Hold FADER and tap a pad to reassign it. Labels above the pads show the options.
■
Shift — secondary functions on almost every button. If something isn't working as expected, check whether you need to add or remove Shift.
■
Main — home. Press it from anywhere to return to the top-level project view. Safe to press at any time.
■
Commit — duplicates the current scene and steps into it, leaving the original untouched. The most important creative button on the device.
The Display
The 3-character readout shows values and short mode labels — 128 = current BPM, A01 = Group A Pattern 1, S03 = Scene 3. The backlit icons surrounding it give context: which icon is lit tells you what the number refers to. When in doubt, look at which icon is active before turning a knob.
Watch Out
⚠️
Knob X and Y do different things in different modes. There's no fixed answer to "what does Knob X do?" — it always depends on context. The display and icons tell you what's active before you turn anything.
Lesson 1.2

Groups, Patterns & Scenes

The three-level hierarchy that everything on the EP-133 is built around. Get this mental model clear and nothing about the device will confuse you.

The Hierarchy
1
Pattern — a looping sequence of notes for one group. Group A might have Pattern 1 = main drum loop, Pattern 2 = breakdown version. Each group has 99 patterns.
2
Scene — a snapshot of which pattern is playing in each group simultaneously. Scene 1: A-P1 + B-P1 + C-P1. Scene 2: A-P1 + B-P1 + C-P2 (chord variation). Up to 99 scenes.
3
Project — the whole thing. All groups, all patterns, all scenes. Up to 9 projects on the device. One project = one track or one idea.
Navigating
→
Switch patterns within a group: hold the Group button + press – or +.
→
Switch scenes: hold Main + press – or +. Or hold Main and tap a numbered pad to jump directly.
→
Undo: Shift + Group B. The umbrella icon lights when undo is available.
Tip
💡
Patterns vs Scenes is the most common early confusion. Switching patterns (hold Group + –/+) only changes that one group. Switching scenes (hold Main + –/+) changes all four groups at once. Know which you're doing before pressing.
Practice
Exercise 1.2

Navigate Without Breaking Anything

Pure navigation — no recording. Build the muscle memory now.

  • Hold Main + press + twice → Scene 3. Hold Main + tap pad 1 → back to Scene 1.
  • Press Shift + Group B → Undo. Does the umbrella icon appear?
  • Press Main → confirm you're back at the home view.

Steps active: 1, 5, 9, 13 — one kick per quarter note, four per bar. "Around the World" runs this pattern for almost seven minutes straight and never gets boring, because everything else around it is doing the work. When you program your first four-on-the-floor on the EP-133, you're using the exact rhythmic idea that anchors one of the most-played dance records ever made.

1

2

3
Release RECORD. Listen back. The kick should land rock-solid on every beat.
Tip
💡

Lesson 2.1

How Sampling Works on the EP-133

Before you record a single sound, understand the path it takes — from source, through the device, onto a pad. This mental model prevents most beginner confusion.

Explanation
Sampling on the EP-133 is a three-step process: record audio into one of 999 sample slots, trim it to exactly the portion you want, then assign it to a pad so it plays back when tapped. The device has 128MB of onboard memory shared across all 999 slots — more than enough for a full track's worth of material. Audio can come from two sources: the built-in microphone (pointing upward along the top edge) or the 3.5mm line input. When a cable is plugged into the input, it takes priority over the mic automatically. Samples are stored in the device's memory even after power off — they persist until you delete them or load a new project.
The Sample Flow
1
Source — mic (ambient sound, singing, beatboxing, foley) or line-in (records, phones, synths, other hardware). Choose based on what you're capturing.
2
Record — press SAMPLE to enter sample mode, then hold RECORD to capture audio. The EP-133 records into a buffer first, giving you the option to trim before committing.
3
Trim — use the – and + buttons along with Knob X/Y to set the start and end points. This is where you isolate the exact hit, loop, or phrase you want.
4
Assign — tap a pad to assign the trimmed sample to it. That pad now plays your sample. It will stay assigned until you overwrite it.
5
Play — the assigned pad plays the sample at its natural pitch and length when tapped. Velocity and pressure change how it plays. Further tweaks (pitch, loop mode, FX) come in later modules.
Real-World Reference
Music Has the Right to Children — Boards of Canada (1998)
Warp Records · Electronic / Ambient · Various BPM
Boards of Canada built their entire early catalogue by recording ambient sounds — degraded cassette recordings, found audio, field recordings, childhood home movies — trimming them to usable fragments, and building hypnotic loops from the results. "Music Has the Right to Children" is essentially a sampling masterclass disguised as an album. The EP-133's workflow of record → trim → assign mirrors exactly what they were doing with far more cumbersome equipment. The difference is your source material. Start with whatever's around you: your voice, a window opening, a kettle boiling, a finger snapping. The process is identical no matter what you record.
Tip
💡
Record longer than you need, then trim down. It's much easier to trim a sample shorter than to re-record because you cut off too early. When in doubt, start recording a beat before the sound begins and stop a beat after it ends. You can always trim the excess.
Watch Out
⚠️
Sample slots are per-group, per-sound. Each group (A–D) has 12 sound slots (one per pad). When you assign a sample to pad 3 in Group A, it replaces whatever was there before. If you're using factory sounds and want to keep them, assign your new samples to empty slots in a different group or pattern.
Practice

Lesson 2.2

Recording from the Mic & Line-In

Getting clean audio into the EP-133 is a skill in itself. Input level, mic placement, and source selection each affect the quality of everything you sample.

Explanation
The EP-133's built-in microphone is an upward-facing omnidirectional mic — it hears everything around it, not just what's directly above. This makes it great for capturing ambient textures and room sounds, but it also means background noise easily bleeds in. For cleaner recordings of specific sounds, get the device as close to the source as possible, or use the line-in instead. The line input accepts a 3.5mm stereo signal and gives you a much cleaner, isolated capture — ideal for sampling from a phone, record player, or synthesizer. Input level is critical: too low = noisy sample; too high = digital clipping. Watch the level indicator on the display and aim for peaks in the upper-middle range.
Step-by-Step — Recording a Sample
1
Press SAMPLE to enter sample mode. The display confirms you're ready to record.
2
If using line-in: plug your source into the 3.5mm input jack. Set your source volume to around 70% to start.
3
Hold RECORD to begin capturing. Make your sound — speak, clap, play the source. Release RECORD when done.
4
The EP-133 immediately shows you the captured audio. Press PLAY to audition what you recorded before trimming.
5
If the level is too quiet or too loud, press ERASE to discard and re-record. Adjust your source volume or mic distance and try again.
Real-World Reference
Demon Days — Gorillaz (2005)
Parlophone · Alternative / Hip-Hop / Electronic · Various BPM
Danger Mouse assembled "Demon Days" using a combination of live recordings and sampled material — some captured cleanly in the studio, others deliberately lo-fi and degraded. Tracks like "Feel Good Inc." layer tightly recorded bass and vocals against grainy, textured samples. The contrast between clean and dirty sources is intentional and expressive. On the EP-133, you have the same choice: the line-in gives you studio-clean capture, the mic gives you roomy, imperfect texture. Neither is wrong. The question is which serves the sound you're building.
Tip
💡
For mic recordings, cup your hands around the device. The EP-133's mic picks up everything — including reflections off your desk. Holding the device up and cupping both hands loosely around it blocks reflections and focuses the mic toward whatever's in front of you, giving noticeably cleaner results in live environments.
Watch Out
⚠️
Clipping sounds bad and can't be fixed after recording. A clipped sample has a harsh, crackling distortion on loud transients that no amount of trimming or processing will remove. If your recording sounds harsh or distorted on playback, the input level was too hot. Discard it, lower the source volume, and re-record.
Practice
Exercise 2.2

Your First Three Samples

Record three sounds using the built-in mic. Don't worry about what they are — focus on getting clean, unclipped recordings each time.

  • Sample 3: an ambient sound from the room. Let the mic capture whatever's there for 3 seconds.
  • Audition each one using Play. Which sounds cleanest? Which has the most character?
Lesson 2.3

Trimming & Setting Loop Points

A raw recording is just raw material. Trimming is where you turn it into something usable — cutting out silence, isolating the exact hit, and deciding whether it loops.

Explanation
After recording, the EP-133 puts you directly into trim mode. You see a waveform representation on the display and use – / + along with Knob X (start point) and Knob Y (end point) to define exactly which portion of the recording plays back. The start point is critical for percussive sounds: any silence before the transient makes pads feel sluggish and unresponsive. Trim it tight to the very beginning of the sound. For loops — a phrase you want to repeat seamlessly — the end point needs to align cleanly so the loop doesn't stutter or click at the join. This takes practice and a careful ear.
Step-by-Step — Trimming a One-Shot
1
After recording, you're automatically in trim view. Press PLAY to hear the full raw recording with current start/end points.
2
Turn Knob X to move the start point — push it right until the leading silence is eliminated. The sound should begin the instant the pad is tapped.
3
Turn Knob Y to move the end point — pull it left to cut off any trailing silence or unwanted sound after the hit.
4
Press PLAY again to audition the trimmed result. Repeat adjustments until the sample plays exactly as intended.
5
Tap a pad to assign the trimmed sample. Done — it's now live on that pad.
Real-World Reference
Come to Daddy — Aphex Twin (1997)
Warp Records · Electronic / Industrial · Various BPM
Aphex Twin's approach to sampling was notoriously surgical — sounds trimmed to single-millisecond precision, transients sharpened, loops cut so tightly they feel like they breathe. "Come to Daddy" layers organic vocal fragments against processed percussion, and the precision of those edits is what makes it feel so unsettling. Even at its most chaotic, the underlying sample work is extremely exact. Good trimming is invisible — you only notice bad trimming (the click, the gap, the late start). Get your start points tight, and the rest of the creative work has a solid foundation.
Tip
💡
For loops, listen for clicks at the join point. A click means the waveform doesn't land at zero-crossing at the end — the audio is abruptly cut. Move the end point slightly in either direction using SHIFT + Knob Y for fine adjustment until the click disappears and the loop seams seamlessly.
Watch Out
⚠️
Don't trim too aggressively on sounds with a slow attack. Pads, chords, and sustained sounds often have a gentle fade-in at the start. If you trim too tight on these, you'll cut off the beginning of the sound and it'll feel harsh. Give slow-attack sounds a little breathing room at the start point.
Practice
Exercise 2.3

The Tight Trim Challenge

Record a hand clap and trim it as tightly as possible. The goal: zero gap between pad tap and sound.

  • Record a sharp hand clap using the mic
  • In trim mode, push the start point right until just before the transient spike
  • Assign it to a pad and tap it repeatedly — does it feel instant?
  • If there's any delay before the sound, trim the start point tighter
  • Now try on a longer sound (a hummed note) — where would you set the end point to make it feel musical?
Lesson 2.4

Assigning Samples to Pads

The pad layout is your instrument. How you arrange samples across the 12 pads — and across the four groups — determines how naturally you can perform and build patterns.

Explanation
Each group (A–D) has 12 pads, each of which holds one sample. Assignment is simple: after trimming, tap the pad you want the sample on. But thinking about which pad to assign it to is worth a moment's thought. Convention on the EP-133 is to put drums in Group A — and within Group A, to put the kick on pad 1, snare on pad 2, hi-hats on pads 3 and 4. This mirrors the layout of hardware drum machines (Roland TR-series, etc.) and makes programming patterns feel intuitive. Groups B, C, and D are then free for bass, chords, and textures. You can also transfer samples from your computer via USB using Teenage Engineering's EP Sample Tool — useful for loading in specific sounds, loops, or breaks you've prepared in advance.
Suggested Pad Layout
A
Group A — Drums: Pad 1 = Kick, Pad 2 = Snare, Pad 3 = Closed Hat, Pad 4 = Open Hat, Pad 5 = Clap, Pads 6–12 = percussion, FX, additional hits.
B
Group B — Bass: Pads 1–12 = bass notes or bass one-shots arranged chromatically or by root note. Bass samples pitched across pads give you a playable bass instrument.
C
Group C — Chords / Melody: Sampled chords, stabs, vocal phrases, or melodic one-shots. One pad per sound or musical idea.
D
Group D — Textures / FX: Loops, ambience, risers, FX hits, anything that doesn't fit cleanly in A, B, or C. Use this group for colour and atmosphere.
Real-World Reference
Discovery — Daft Punk (2001)
Virgin Records · French House / Electro · Various BPM
"Discovery" was built by methodically assigning sampled elements across hardware — specific drum hits to specific pads, specific chord samples to specific keys, specific bass tones to specific triggers. Daft Punk's genius wasn't just in their sample choices but in their arrangement of those samples into a performable, improvable system. Every pad was a deliberate decision. "Digital Love," "Harder Better Faster Stronger," "Instant Crush" — all of them start with a well-organised set of samples on well-organised pads, then use sequencing and live performance to build something greater. Think about your pad layout as your instrument design — before a single note is played.
Tip
💡
Be consistent with your pad layout across projects. If kick is always pad 1 and snare is always pad 2, your hands learn the layout and you stop thinking about it. Muscle memory in pad position is one of the most valuable skills you can build — consistency is what creates it.
Watch Out
⚠️
Assigning a sample overwrites whatever was there. If you accidentally assign a new recording over a pad that had a sound you wanted to keep, press SHIFT + Group B immediately to undo. Beyond that, the previous assignment is gone. Make sure you're tapping the right pad before confirming an assignment.
Practice

Lesson 2.5

Playback Modes & Pitch

Once a sample is on a pad, you have significant control over how it plays back. Pitch, loop mode, and one-shot vs. gate behaviour each change the creative possibilities of the same recording.

Explanation
The EP-133 gives you several ways to control how a sample plays. In Sound Edit mode (hold SHIFT + press SOUND), you can adjust the pitch of each sample — useful for tuning a bass hit to a specific key, or detuning a sample for effect. You can also set whether the sample plays as a one-shot (plays fully regardless of how long you hold the pad) or a gate (plays only while the pad is held). One-shot is standard for drum hits; gate is useful for sustained sounds or vocals you want to control manually. The Keys mode lets you play a single sample chromatically across all 12 pads — turning any sample into a pitched instrument.
The Three Key Playback Controls
1
Pitch — in Sound Edit mode, Knob X adjusts pitch in semitones. Knob Y adjusts fine-tuning (cents). Use this to tune bass samples to your track's key, or to deliberately detune for texture.
2
One-shot vs Gate — one-shot plays the full sample on a single tap; gate plays only while the pad is held. Set per-pad in Sound Edit mode. A kick should almost always be one-shot. A sustained chord or vocal sample benefits from gate.
3
Keys mode (KEYS button) — maps a single sample across all 12 pads in semitone steps. Tap pad 1 = root pitch, pad 2 = one semitone up, and so on. This turns any sample — a bass hit, a vocal snippet, a synth stab — into a fully playable melodic instrument.
Real-World Reference
Homework — Daft Punk (1997)
Virgin Records · French House / Electronic · Various BPM
Daft Punk's "Homework" is a clinic in pitch-shifted sampling. Tracks like "Da Funk" and "Rollin' & Scratchin'" take single recorded sounds and pitch them, loop them, and layer them until they become something entirely new. "Around the World"'s bassline is a single sampled bass hit pitched chromatically across a sequence — the same sample, different pitches, creating melody. The EP-133's Keys mode lets you do this immediately: record one bass hit, enter Keys mode, and suddenly you have a 12-note bass instrument built from a single recording. This is a core technique — one sample, infinite musical material.
Tip
💡
Use Keys mode on everything, not just melodic samples. Try it on a drum hit — pitching a snare up creates a higher-pitched rimshot; pitching it down creates a deep, almost kick-like thud. A single recorded drum sound in Keys mode gives you an entire percussive instrument with 12 tonal variations. This is one of the fastest ways to add melodic interest to a drum-only pattern.
Watch Out
⚠️
Pitching a sample up stretches its perceived speed, pitching down stretches its length. A heavily pitched-up sample may sound thin and time-stretched; a pitched-down sample plays back slower and longer. This is especially noticeable on samples with distinct rhythmic content. Use Shift + Knob for fine-pitch adjustments to avoid large, unnatural-sounding shifts unless that's the effect you're going for.
Practice
Exercise 2.5

One Sample, Three Instruments

Record a single sound and use it three different ways. This demonstrates how much creative material lives inside a single recording.

  • Record a bass-heavy sound — a deep hum, a chest thump, or a low vocal note

Lesson 3.1

Live Recording & the Count-In

Live recording is the fastest way to get ideas down. You play — the EP-133 captures. The four-beat count-in is your runway. Once you trust it, your recordings get tighter immediately.

Explanation
The EP-133 has two ways to start recording. The first — holding RECORD and tapping pads — drops you straight in with no count-in, useful for quick punch-ins over an existing pattern. The second — tapping RECORD then PLAY — gives you a four-beat count-in before recording begins. This is the one to use for laying down a new pattern from scratch. The count-in lets you find the tempo in your body before the first beat lands. Once the pattern is looping, every pass is an overdub — pads you play are added to what's already there. Press PLAY to stop playback entirely, or press RECORD alone to keep the loop running but exit record mode so you can listen back.
The Two Recording Modes
1
Count-in mode: tap RECORD, then tap PLAY. You'll hear four beats before recording starts. Best for recording a fresh pattern — gives your hands time to find the groove.
2
Instant punch-in: hold RECORD and hit pads immediately. No count-in — notes record the moment you touch a pad. Best for adding a single element to a pattern that's already playing.
3
Exit record, keep playing: press RECORD once while the loop is running. Recording stops, playback continues. You can now listen critically before deciding what to add next.
4
Stop everything: press PLAY. Playback and recording both halt. The pattern is preserved exactly as it was when you stopped.
Real-World Reference
Homework — Daft Punk (1997)
Virgin Records · French House · Various BPM
Daft Punk have described their early recording process as loop-based and iterative — starting a groove, playing over it, then adding one element at a time until the track found its shape. Tracks like "Da Funk" and "Around the World" weren't built in one take; they were assembled pass by pass. The EP-133's overdub model — where each pass adds to the last — is exactly that workflow. Tap Record then Play, find your tempo in the count-in, play the kick. Listen. Punch in the snare. Listen again. This is how the music gets made: one layer at a time, judging each one before committing the next.
Tip
💡
Use the count-in to feel the tempo, not just count it. Don't use the four beats to mentally count "1-2-3-4." Use them to nod your head, tap your foot, let the BPM settle into your body. When the recording starts, your first hit should land naturally — not because you were counting, but because you felt when it was time.
Watch Out
⚠️
Every overdub pass adds — it doesn't replace. If you re-enter record mode on a pattern that already has notes, everything you play is layered on top. If you want to re-record a specific pad from scratch, hold ERASE + tap that pad first to clear its notes, then punch in your new recording.
Practice
Exercise 3.1

Three-Pass Build

Build a complete groove using three separate recording passes with the count-in. No step sequencing — all live.

  • Set BPM to 120. Tap Record then Play — use the count-in to feel the tempo.
  • Pass 1: record kick on every beat. Press Record to stop recording, listen to one full loop.
  • Pass 2: hold Record to punch in — add snare on beats 2 and 4. Exit record, listen.
  • Pass 3: count-in again — add hi-hats on the offbeats. Press Play to stop.
  • Listen to the result. Is the timing tight? If any element feels off, erase just that pad and re-record it.
Lesson 3.2

Step Sequencing

Step sequencing is the other way in. Instead of playing in real time, you move through the pattern one step at a time and place notes exactly where you want them. Precise, methodical, and great for rhythms that are hard to play live.

Explanation
In step sequencing mode, you navigate the pattern grid using – and + to move between steps, and hold RECORD while tapping a pad to place a note at the current step position. The display shows your current step position as you move through the bar. This approach is slower than live recording but gives you surgical precision — perfect for programming syncopated rhythms, fast 32nd-note patterns, or anything where live timing is difficult. You can also use step mode to edit individual hits in an existing pattern: navigate to any step, then hold ERASE + tap the pad to remove just that one hit, or hold RECORD + tap to add one.
Step-by-Step
1
Stop playback. Press – or + to navigate to Step 1 — the display shows the current step position.
2
Hold RECORD and tap a pad to place a note at the current step. Release RECORD.
3
Press + to move to the next step. Hold RECORD + tap another pad (or the same pad) to place the next note.
4
Continue moving through the bar, placing notes step by step. Press PLAY at any point to hear what you've built so far.
5
To remove a single note: navigate to its step, hold ERASE + tap the pad. That specific hit is removed; everything else stays.

Lesson 3.3

Timing, Quantise & Swing

Timing is where feel lives. Perfectly quantised patterns can sound lifeless. Unquantised ones can sound sloppy. The EP-133 gives you fine control over both — and a swing setting that splits the difference.

Explanation
The TIMING button controls two things: the note interval (how fine-grained the sequencer grid is) and whether quantise is on or off. Press TIMING and turn Knob X to change the note interval — the default is 1/16, giving you 16 steps per bar. Moving to 1/32 doubles the resolution for finer edits; 1/8 gives you a coarser, faster grid. With quantise off, notes are recorded at their exact played timing — giving your pattern a human, slightly loose feel. With quantise on, every hit snaps to the nearest grid division. Timing Correct is the in-between: during playback, hold a pad while holding TIMING to selectively quantise just that pad's notes — the display shows how many notes were corrected. Swing is applied via Knob Y in the timing menu and pushes every other 16th-note back slightly, creating the characteristic shuffle feel of classic drum machines.
Timing Controls
1
Note interval: press TIMING, turn Knob X. Options: 1/1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/8T, 1/16, 1/16T, 1/32. Start at 1/16 for most patterns.
2
Quantise on/off: press TIMING to toggle. When on, live-recorded hits snap to the grid. When off, hits land at their exact played moment — including any early or late timing.
3
Timing Correct (selective quantise): during playback, hold TIMING + hold a pad. The display counts how many notes on that pad were snapped to the grid. Release to apply.
4
Swing: press TIMING, turn Knob Y. Increases the delay on every other 16th note. Low swing = tight. High swing = shuffled, triplet-like feel. Classic drum machine territory.
5
Nudge individual notes: while stopped, hold SHIFT + a pad, then press – / + to nudge that note forward or back in ticks. Ticks are the smallest time unit on the EP-133 — finer than any note interval.
Real-World Reference
Music Has the Right to Children — Boards of Canada (1998)
Warp Records · Electronic / Ambient · Various BPM
Boards of Canada are famous for deliberately detuning their samples and slightly desynchronising their sequencer timing to create a feeling of memory and decay — music that sounds like it's being remembered rather than played. Their hi-hats often sit fractionally off the grid, their kicks feel slightly heavy rather than laser-precise. You don't need expensive tools to get this effect. On the EP-133: record with quantise off, then use Timing Correct to quantise only the kick (tightening the foundation) while leaving the hi-hats and snare with their natural human imprecision. The result sounds alive rather than mechanical.
Tip
💡
Swing between 54–62% is the sweet spot for most styles. Below 50% there's no swing. Above 66% it starts to sound lurching and overly shuffled. House music typically sits around 55–58%. Hip-hop grooves push toward 60–65%. Start at 55%, listen for a full 8 bars, and adjust from there — your ear will tell you when it's right.
Watch Out
⚠️
Swing affects the whole pattern, not individual pads. There's no per-pad swing setting — if you increase swing, every element in that group shifts. If you want your kick quantised tight but your hi-hat swung, you'll need to put them in separate groups and set swing differently per group, or nudge individual notes manually with Shift + pad + –/+.
Practice
Exercise 3.3

Feel the Difference: Quantised vs Swung

Build the same pattern three ways and listen to how timing changes the feel of identical notes.

  • Record a kick/snare/hat pattern with quantise ON. Listen for 8 bars. Notice how mechanical it feels.
  • Erase all notes. Turn quantise OFF. Record the same pattern live — let your natural timing land. Listen for 8 bars. Notice where it feels loose or expressive.
  • Apply Timing Correct to just the kick pad (hold Timing + kick pad during playback). Listen — kick is now tight, other elements keep their feel.
  • Add swing at 56%. Listen again. Same notes, completely different groove.
Lesson 3.4

Pattern Length & Polyrhythm

Not everything has to be one bar. Longer patterns give you room to build evolving sequences. And because each group has its own independent length, the EP-133 naturally creates polyrhythmic tension that repeats on longer cycles.

Explanation
By default, new patterns on the EP-133 are one bar long. To extend: hold RECORD and press + to add bars, one at a time. Each group can have a different pattern length — Group A's drums might loop every 2 bars, Group B's bassline every 4 bars, and Group C's chord stab every 3 bars. Because they're different lengths, they fall in and out of phase with each other, creating complex, shifting musical textures that don't repeat until all three lengths have completed a full common cycle. This is polyrhythm — and it's a powerful compositional tool that happens almost automatically on the EP-133 just by setting different lengths per group.
Setting Pattern Length
1
To extend a pattern: hold RECORD + press +. Each press adds one bar. The display shows the new length (e.g. Ln.2 = 2 bars).
2
To shorten: hold RECORD + press –. Notes recorded beyond the new end point are not deleted — they'll reappear if you extend the pattern again.
3
To set different lengths per group: select each group in turn (press Group A, B, C, D) and set its length independently using hold Record + +/–.
4
Polyrhythm example: Group A = 2 bars (drum loop), Group B = 3 bars (bass phrase), Group C = 4 bars (chord progression). They'll sync back to a shared downbeat every 12 bars — the pattern feels new for longer before it repeats.
Real-World Reference
Kid A — Radiohead (2000)
Parlophone · Electronic / Art Rock · Various BPM
Radiohead's "Kid A" was heavily influenced by the polyrhythmic sequencing of bands like Can and the minimalist composers — patterns of different lengths running simultaneously, falling in and out of phase with each other. "Everything in Its Right Place" uses a chord cycle that doesn't sync neatly with its underlying rhythm, creating a feeling of restless, floating movement. The EP-133 produces this naturally: set Group A to 2 bars, Group B to 3 bars, press Play and just listen. The patterns interact in ways you didn't compose deliberately — they find combinations you wouldn't have thought of. That's a feature, not a bug.
Tip
💡
Use a 2-bar drum pattern as your default. A single-bar drum loop repeats every bar and can start to feel robotic fast. A 2-bar pattern lets you put a variation in the second bar — an extra snare hit, a missing hi-hat, a ghost note — that makes the groove feel alive without needing complex programming. The difference between 1-bar and 2-bar drum patterns is enormous.
Watch Out
⚠️
If your bassline feels like it's cutting off early, check the pattern length. The most common cause of a bassline that stops before it finishes is that Group B's pattern length is still set to 1 bar but you recorded a phrase longer than that. Hold Record + press + to extend Group B's length until it matches your bassline's full phrase.
Practice
Exercise 3.4

The 2+3 Polyrhythm

Set up a deliberate polyrhythm between two groups and listen to what emerges.

  • Group A: record a 2-bar drum loop (kick, snare, hats). Set length to 2 bars.
  • Group B: record a 3-bar bassline — a simple 3-note phrase that takes 3 bars to complete. Set length to 3 bars.
  • Press Play. Listen for 12+ bars — how long before the two patterns sync back to a shared downbeat?
  • Add Group C with a 4-bar chord pattern. Now the full repeat cycle is every 12 bars.
  • Notice which bar combinations feel like natural "drops" — those are your scene transition points.
Lesson 3.5

Note Repeat & Fills

Note Repeat is the EP-133's fastest tool for building energy. Hold a pad and the device fires it repeatedly at the current note interval — turning a single press into a hi-hat roll, a snare fill, or a rapid-fire melodic phrase.

Explanation
Note Repeat is activated by holding TIMING + holding a pad. While both are held, the device fires that pad repeatedly at the current timing interval. Switch timing to 1/16 for a straight 16th-note repeat. Switch to 1/8T (triplet) for a rolling, shuffled feel. Switch to 1/32 for a rapid fire effect on drops and fills. You can change the note interval while holding the pad — the repeat rate changes in real time. Combine this with the fader (controlling volume) for rolls that swell in and out. You can also latch note repeat: hold SHIFT + TIMING to lock it on, freeing both hands for other controls.
Using Note Repeat
1
Set your note interval: press TIMING + Knob X. 1/16 is the standard starting point for hi-hat rolls.
2
Hold TIMING + hold a pad. The device fires that pad at the current note interval for as long as you hold. Release either to stop.
3
While holding, change the interval in real time: press TIMING + Knob X with your other hand. The repeat rate shifts immediately — ramp up to 1/32 for a fill that builds.
4
Latch mode: press SHIFT + TIMING to lock Note Repeat on. Now you can remove your hand from the pad — the repeat continues. Press SHIFT + TIMING again to unlock.
5
Record a fill: enter record mode and use Note Repeat to play a fill live — the repeated notes are captured into the pattern at their exact positions. This is the fastest way to program a 16th-note hi-hat roll.

The One-Bar Fill

Build a 2-bar pattern where bar 1 is your regular groove and bar 2 ends with a Note Repeat fill in the last beat.
